## What it is

Jobs to Be Done (JTBD) reframes discovery around the progress a customer is trying to make in a specific circumstance, rather than who they are demographically. The core idea: people don't buy products, they "hire" them to get a job done — and that product competes against every other way the job could get done, including doing nothing at all, using a workaround, or hiring a competitor.

A job statement usually follows a simple structure: *"When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]."* For example: "When I'm switching tools mid-project, I want to migrate my data without losing history, so I can keep my team's trust in the new system."

## When to use it

JTBD interviews are most valuable early in discovery, when you're trying to understand *why* customers switched to your product, stuck with an old one, or churned. It's especially useful for finding the real trigger behind a purchase decision — the moment of friction or frustration that made someone start looking for a new solution — which persona-based research often misses because it segments by who the customer is, not what they're struggling to accomplish.

## Common pitfalls

- **Job statements that are really feature requests in disguise.** "I want a dashboard" is a solution, not a job — the underlying job might be "I want to know if I'm on track without asking my manager."
- **Skipping the forces of progress.** JTBD interviews are built to surface the push (dissatisfaction with the old way), pull (appeal of the new way), and anxieties/habits holding someone back — skip these and you're left with a vague need statement instead of an actionable insight.
- **Treating JTBD as a replacement for all other research.** It's a strong lens for switching behavior and motivation, not a substitute for usability testing or quantitative validation.
